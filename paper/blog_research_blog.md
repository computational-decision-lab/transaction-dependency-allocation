# Who Pays for the Parent? Transaction Dependency, Blockspace Allocation, and Confirmation-Delay Redistribution in Bitcoin

When blockspace is scarce, a high-fee Bitcoin transaction can help its low-fee parent confirm while an unrelated transaction waits longer. What's less obvious—and what matters for wallet engineers, protocol researchers, and anyone building on Bitcoin—is whether that reshuffling changes the *overall* distribution of delay, or just swaps identities around a fixed queue.

This study isolates that question with valid signed transactions executed in Bitcoin Core v31.0 on Regtest. Its original 800-fixture matrix compares three selection rules on matched dependency graphs. Follow-up experiments vary transaction sizes and arrival schedules, expand selected cells to 100 fee seeds, and compare actual prior-version templates. Together they test when dependency changes *who* waits and when it changes the aggregate distribution. The outcomes are controlled synthetic measurements; no Mainnet transaction history or user welfare is observed.

## The Experimental Design

We built 800 fixtures by crossing 40 synthetic fee seeds, five graph structures (independent transactions, Child Pays for Parent (CPFP) pairs, four-node chains, five-child stars, and mixed branches), and four block capacities (2, 4, 6, and 12 transaction slots). Each fixture contains twelve legally signed Taproot transactions—326 vBytes each, one input, six outputs—with fees, weights, and arrival times matched across graph settings. The transactions arrive simultaneously, then eight blocks are scheduled at 600-second intervals.

Three selectors process each fixture. The first is a scalar rule that ranks currently parent-eligible transactions by individual fee rate—a research counterfactual, not a historical Core implementation. The second uses ancestor-package scoring, recomputing effective rates for linked transactions. The third is actual Bitcoin Core `getblocktemplate`, with its cluster mempool logic and topological constraints intact. All resulting blocks are generated and validated by the same Core node. That yields 2,400 selector trajectories, each producing a sequence of confirmed transaction identities and timestamps.

The graph structures matter. In the independent case, all twelve transactions are unrelated; there's no dependency to exploit. In the CPFP pair, a high-fee child can lift a low-fee parent. The chain places that high-fee child immediately after the root, with two further descendants whose fees follow the seeded assignment. The star gives the same parent five children, with one deliberately high-fee child and four other seeded fees. Six transactions remain unrelated singletons across all graph settings, serving as a control cohort.

We report paired comparisons on fee seeds—10,000 bootstrap draws per comparison—because the same seed generates legally distinct but economically matched transaction sets. The burden measure counts time without confirmation up to the 4,800-second horizon; nonconfirmed transactions receive that full horizon burden. These are controlled Regtest outcomes, not a sample of independent Mainnet transactions.

The original matrix is not the whole evidence base in this revision. We added 480 size-and-arrival fixtures and 300 precision fixtures. The size/arrival diagnostics use ten seeds per cell and are explicitly exploratory; five selected simultaneous cells combine the original forty with sixty additional fee seeds, reaching 100 independent seed units. A separate actual Core v30.2 comparison replays every original signed transaction set.

## What Dependency Actually Does

![Complete reported result surface. Each cell is the Core-minus-scalar contrast for one graph and transaction-slot budget. The three panels retain the unrelated restricted-burden change, first-block fee-capture ratio change, and confirmation-burden Gini change. Positive values mean a larger value under Core; the zero cells at twelve slots are uncongested controls. The surface reports the original 40-seed matched matrix, not additional precision runs.](assets/result_surface.png)

The surface shows where the mechanism is active. Delay and fee-capture contrasts are largest under the smaller budgets, while all twelve-slot independent-transaction controls are exactly zero because the cohort fits in the first block. Gini changes only where the dependency chunk changes the confirmation-count sequence; a nonzero unrelated contrast alone is therefore not evidence of greater aggregate inequality.

![Comparator sensitivity and precision check. The left panel uses the same four-slot chain and plots Core minus scalar against Core minus ancestor-package with a common sign convention: positive means more unrelated restricted waiting under Core. The right panel repeats the Core-minus-scalar chain estimate for the original 40 seeds and the targeted 100-seed extension. Error bars are the reported 95% paired-bootstrap intervals. The ancestor-package estimate is shown only for the reported four-slot chain; no unreported cells are inferred.](assets/comparator_effects.png)

The comparison baseline changes the interpretation. In the four-slot chain, Core minus scalar is +0.583 blocks (95% interval [0.508, 0.658]), whereas Core minus ancestor-package is -0.042 blocks ([-0.079, -0.008]). The 100-seed scalar extension remains positive at +0.552 blocks ([0.508, 0.593]). These are different counterfactual questions, so neither sign should be presented as a baseline-free property of cluster selection.

**Result one**: In a four-slot chain, Core's package logic gives unrelated transactions an additional 350 scheduled seconds of restricted nonconfirmation burden on average (95% interval: 305 to 395 seconds, or about 0.51 to 0.66 blocks) compared to the scalar counterfactual. First-block fees rise by 8,369.5 sat on average. But here's the key: *aggregate mean delay and Gini change by exactly zero*. Both selectors confirm four transactions per block for three blocks, so the multiset of waiting times is identical—only the identities differ. The chain's parent confirms sooner on average; unrelated transactions wait longer.

This is not a new discovery about Child Pays for Parent (CPFP). CPFP and package relay are established Bitcoin mechanisms. The contribution here is *quantifying the redistribution* in a controlled setting and distinguishing identity reshuffling from aggregate distributional change.

**Result two**: That zero aggregate change is not an accident. When a cohort of transactions arrives simultaneously, with fixed block times and a fixed observation horizon, identical per-block confirmation counts mathematically guarantee identical aggregate mean delay and Gini—even when individual identities shift. It's a simple accounting proposition. Example: four equal-size transactions with rates 2 sat/vB (parent), 100 (child), 40, and 39 (unrelated), two slots per block. A scalar rule selects the unrelated pair first. A package selector can choose parent-plus-child. Both produce the confirmation-time multiset {1, 1, 2, 2} blocks, but the unrelated pair waits an extra block in the package case. Same aggregate statistics, different winners. This is an illustration, not an estimate of a typical Bitcoin outcome.

**Result three**: The five-child star breaks that symmetry. With four slots, unrelated transactions bear +327.5 seconds of additional burden under Core versus scalar, *and* aggregate mean burden rises by +200 seconds. Gini increases by 0.018; confirmation incidence drops by 0.0625. Some dependency chunks exceed the entire synthetic block capacity and remain unconfirmed at the horizon, changing the cohort's confirmation counts. The effect is not a uniform delay for every transaction. The two-slot case is more extreme: the star parent's burden increases by 1,740 seconds even though its initial dependency premium is positive. This is a deliberate tiny-capacity stress boundary—normal Mainnet blocks are much larger than a default cluster size. It provides no evidence of routine Mainnet starvation or a protocol defect.

**Result four**: Baseline choice matters. Hold Core fixed and compare the dependent star to a legally regenerated independent graph with matched fees and sizes. In this four-slot comparison, the unrelated-burden contrast is *negative* 80 seconds (95% interval: -157.5 to -5 seconds). The sign flips. Changing the policy on a given graph is not the same as changing the graph under a fixed policy. Both comparisons are valid; they answer different questions.

**Result five**: Even when aggregate statistics align, revenue and waiting can trade off. Compare Core to the ancestor-package counterfactual in the four-slot chain: unrelated burden decreases by 25 seconds, but first-block fees drop by 1,456.775 sat on average. The relative performance depends on both the comparator and the measured outcome. These fee and delay measurements do not identify an economically optimal policy without user values and other costs.

**Result six**: All original equal-size, simultaneous independent-transaction controls show zero selector differences. At twelve slots, all twelve transactions confirm in the first block; there is no difference in delay, fees, utilization or Gini when all demand fits. Snapshot dependency premiums can still differ.

**Result seven**: The targeted precision runs preserve the four-slot chain pattern at 100 fee seeds: Core minus scalar increases unrelated restricted waiting by 0.552 blocks (95% interval: 0.508 to 0.593) and first-block fees by 8,055 sat (5,822 to 10,431). Mean and Gini remain unchanged. In the four-slot star, unrelated burden rises by 0.560 blocks (0.490 to 0.630) and Gini by 0.010 (0.003 to 0.018). The six-slot chain's unrelated contrast is 0.310 blocks (0.273 to 0.347). Independent controls stay exactly at zero. These intervals resample the 100 seed-level paired differences; they do not treat individual transactions or bootstrap draws as new runs.

**Result eight**: The added ten-seed diagnostic reveals an important boundary. In the four-slot chain with equal-size simultaneous arrivals, the unrelated contrast is +0.550 blocks. With heterogeneous sizes it is −0.150 blocks, with an interval from −0.300 to 0.000; full-cohort Gini rises by 0.009. Under two-batch arrivals, equal-size and heterogeneous-size contrasts are +0.317 and −0.683 blocks, respectively, and the Gini contrast is positive in both. Fees remain matched by label, but changing size also changes individual fee rates and which transactions fit. Staggering arrivals also changes time since arrival. These small diagnostics do not establish a general reversal; they show that the equal-size, simultaneous result does not carry over mechanically.

## An Actual Earlier-Version Comparator

The official Bitcoin Core v30.2 binary was checked against its release checksum and eight builder signatures, then given the same 9,600 archived signed transactions and funding chain as the original experiment. Its initial template selected the same set as v31.0 in 751 of 800 fixtures. The remaining 49 selected different sets. Across these fixtures the v31.0-minus-v30.2 first-fee contrast is −1,456.775 sat for the four-slot chain and −6,139.825 sat for the four-slot star, but +488.4 sat for the six-slot star. The first-block optimal-fee gap changes in the same capacity-dependent way.

In the four-unit chain, v31.0 collected 1,456.775 sat less in the first block on average. Relative to the exact dependency- and capacity-feasible optimum, its mean fee gap was 1.817%, compared with 0.646% for v30.2. In the four-unit star the gaps were 7.208% and 2.367%; in the six-unit star v31.0 instead earned 488.4 sat more, with gaps of 0.227% and 0.477%. The direction depends on capacity and topology.

This is an actual version comparison, but it is limited to initial templates. It does not measure v30 confirmation delays, and changes between whole software releases cannot all be attributed to cluster mempool. The weight budgets are also much smaller than normal Mainnet blocks.

## What This Means for Builders

Wallet engineers: standalone fee-rate estimation can miss dependency context. A transaction with a mediocre individual rate might confirm faster than expected if a descendant pays generously—or slower if competing dependency clusters crowd the mempool. This motivates testing graph-aware estimators; the experiment does not measure their production accuracy or savings.

Researchers: declare your counterfactual explicitly and measure both individual outcomes and aggregate distributions. A parent gaining priority is not the same as the system becoming more efficient. Redistribution and aggregate performance need separate measurements.

Protocol designers: every block selection rule embeds tradeoffs. Fee capture, confirmation latency, utilization and transaction-level dispersion need to be reported together. First-block utilization is 100% in every cell of the original equal-size, simultaneous matrix; some penalties emerge later. With heterogeneous sizes or staged arrivals, first-block utilization can also differ. A transaction-level Gini is not inequality among identified users, and a delay change is not a measured welfare loss.

The evidence chain is retained: raw signed transactions, funding inputs, RPC captures and generated blocks. A standalone parser reconstructs fees, graph edges, timestamps and outcomes; a second comparator implementation and an independent optimization solver cross-check the calculations. In a network-disabled, wallet-free environment, the separately signed official Core v31.0 release accepted the original 9,600 transactions and reproduced all 800 initial selected sets. It also replayed all 3,122 candidate blocks across the 780 new fixtures, accepting their 9,360 signed transactions and matching every recorded template order. Eight deliberate mutations of a staged-arrival/heterogeneous-size record were rejected. The independent audit reconstructed all 780 sets and checked their initial fee optima against HiGHS. These tests check archived cases, not every possible Core state. The reproduction archive remains local and has not been publicly published.

This study does not prescribe production strategies. It provides an inspectable controlled benchmark and a useful conceptual distinction: identity reshuffling versus aggregate distributional change. Real Mainnet behavior will differ—dynamic arrivals, strategic fee bidding, miner heterogeneity, and actual user time values all matter. But the core tension remains: under a binding capacity constraint, helping a parent can move another transaction to a later block. Whether that reshuffling improves or degrades the overall waiting distribution depends on topology, capacity, and the selection rule in play.

The useful question is therefore precise: under which dependency structures, capacities, transaction sizes, and arrival schedules does a policy change who waits, and under which conditions does it change the distribution itself? The benchmark makes those questions inspectable without treating either priority gains or extra waiting as the whole result. Its size and arrival extension is deliberately small; observed multi-node mempool histories are the next evidence needed to assess how often these conditions occur in practice.
